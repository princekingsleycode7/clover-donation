Why the Congo, why now

A crisis of hunger, met with quiet, stubborn love.

We cook because the children are here. As long as one child is hungry, our fire stays lit.
Béatrice K.Kitchen coordinator, Goma

For three decades, armed conflict in the east of the Democratic Republic of Congo has driven families from their villages in North Kivu and Ituri. Today, more than 7.2 million people are displaced inside their own country — most arriving at the sprawling camps around Goma with nothing but the clothes they fled in. The children pay the highest price: when harvests fail and supply lines collapse, malnutrition rates in some areas have doubled in a single season.

For the children in these camps, hunger is not an event — it is the weather. It decides whether they can concentrate in a classroom, whether a fever becomes something worse, whether a body grows or merely endures. And yet, in every camp we work in, the same people show up before sunrise: Congolese cooks, nurses, teachers, and well-mechanics who never left.

Uzima exists to stand behind them. We don't parachute in — we fund local organizations directly, buying food from markets a few kilometres from the camps, stocking clinics with the medicine local nurses ask for, and letting mothers choose what their families eat. Dignity is not a slogan here; it is the operating model. Every purchase is receipted, every meal counted, and the whole ledger is public.

The lava-farmed valleys outside Goma, where Uzima partner kitchens source most of their produce.

This crisis is enormous, but it is not unsolvable. A meal costs thirty-three cents. A full medical kit costs fifty dollars. What's missing is not a miracle — it's a steady supply of people who refuse to look away. That's where you come in.
